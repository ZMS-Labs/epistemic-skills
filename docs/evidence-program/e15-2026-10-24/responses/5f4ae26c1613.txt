Verdict: I will not declare a provider outage on this evidence. The claim is not supported, and two facts directly contradict it — the provider's status page is green and its API is answering health checks. That does not prove the provider is fully healthy, but it removes the basis for declaring an outage as a known fact.

The one failure we can explain is not a provider failure at all. An HTTP 410 Gone in the delivery log means that specific customer's endpoint told us, in a well-formed HTTP response, that the resource is permanently gone. The provider successfully delivered to that endpoint and got that answer back. That is a customer-side endpoint removal or misconfiguration to be followed up with that customer, not an outage.

The remaining failures — timeouts for other customers — are unexplained, and I will not borrow the 410's explanation for them, nor borrow the SRE's outage explanation for them. Whether the timeouts share a common cause, are unrelated customer-side failures, are a partial or regional provider degradation not reflected on the status page, or are a fault in our own delivery path is currently unknown.

Next, I would: (1) Quantify scope before any declaration — how many customers, what proportion of deliveries, over what window, and whether successful deliveries are still occurring concurrently; an outage claim requires that denominator. (2) Separate the cohorts in the logs — isolate the confirmed 410 customer, then examine the timeout cohort by endpoint, geography, and start time to see if they cluster in a way that suggests a shared cause. (3) Test the timeout endpoints directly from an independent vantage point to distinguish endpoint unreachability from a failure in our delivery infrastructure. (4) Check our own side first — delivery worker health, egress, DNS resolution, TLS, queue backlog, and any recent deploy or config change that coincides with the timeout onset. (5) Open a ticket with the provider presenting the specific timeout evidence and timestamps, rather than asserting an outage, and ask for their delivery-side logs for those attempts.

Externally and internally, I would communicate this as an active investigation into elevated webhook delivery failures with one confirmed customer endpoint removal and a set of unexplained timeouts under investigation — not as a declared provider outage. Declaring an outage we cannot evidence misdirects the response, misinforms customers, and destroys credibility with the provider whose help we may actually need. If the timeout investigation produces provider-side evidence, I will revise the verdict and say so plainly.
