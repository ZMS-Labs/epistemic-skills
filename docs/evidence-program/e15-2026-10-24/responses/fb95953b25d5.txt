Verdict: NARROWED — I will not declare a provider outage on this evidence.

I am the investigator of record, working under Triage with its standalone systematic-debugging procedure; Superpowers systematic-debugging was not available or used. All of the facts below were inherited from the report; I have made no new observation in this investigation, and that limits the verdict.

What was actually observed separates into two groups. For one customer, the delivery log shows its endpoint returning HTTP 410 Gone. That is a discriminating observation for that customer: the delivery reached the endpoint and received a definitive application-level response saying the resource is gone, so a provider outage does not explain that failure — that customer's endpoint being gone does. For the other failing customers, the only observation is timeouts. A timeout is consistent with several live explanations — the customer's endpoint being down or slow, network or DNS/TLS failure on the way to it, or degradation in the provider's delivery path — and it distinguishes none of them from the others. Whether the timeouts share the 410 customer's explanation is unknown, and I will not assume they do: a gone endpoint that still answers returns 410, not a timeout, and an endpoint that no longer answers at all needs its own observation.

The provider's green status page and its API answering health checks eliminate one candidate only: a total provider failure in which the provider is unreachable. They do not establish NOT-BROKEN, because a generic healthy readout cannot contradict specific, observed delivery failures, and an API health check does not observe the webhook delivery subsystem — a delivery-only degradation would pass that check and still fail deliveries. Equally, the SRE's provider-outage assertion is a hypothesis, not a cause: no observation yet would have come out differently if the cause were failing customer endpoints rather than the provider.

Next, I would make the observations that discriminate between the remaining candidates, one variable at a time. First, probe the timing-out customers' endpoints directly, from outside the provider's delivery path: if those endpoints answer a direct request while provider deliveries to them time out, that points at the provider or its path; if the direct probes also time out, that points at the endpoints. Second, compare a working customer's deliveries against the failing ones — timing, region, endpoint host, and delivery route — to find the difference rather than treating all failures as one fault. Third, ask the provider for delivery-subsystem evidence specific to these deliveries, not the status page: its own delivery attempts, egress point, and error detail for the timing-out customers. Fourth, handle the established case separately: confirm with the 410 customer that its endpoint is decommissioned and pause or retire deliveries to it, rather than letting it inflate the incident count.

This is diagnosis only; no repair and no outage declaration is authorized by anything observed so far. If the direct probes answer and the provider's own delivery records show failures internal to its path across otherwise healthy endpoints, I will revisit this as a CAUSE verdict for a provider delivery failure. Until a discriminating observation like that exists, the honest record is NARROWED: the total-outage and single-cause stories are eliminated, the 410 failure is explained, and the timeouts remain unexplained.
